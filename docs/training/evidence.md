# Evidence base for personal strength training

Checked: 2026-10-19. Scope: targeted research for strength, hypertrophy,
powerlifting, re-entry, workload feedback, nutrition and health. This is not an
exhaustive systematic review. Source IDs resolve to `sources.json`; every card
links to the original publication or official guideline. Reading scopes are
explicit: an indexed abstract is not a full-text appraisal.

## What the evidence supports

An effective program matches the goal, supplies enough repeatable practice and
progressive work, and fits recovery and adherence. There is no sound basis here
to crown one named commercial or competition program the winner for this owner.
Previous success with SRC7 can be useful personal history, but a competition
template needs a separate suitability review when goals or circumstances change.

| Method / variable | Useful role | Limit / current application |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed percentages | Simple, reproducible loading with a credible reference maximum | Guessed maxima and day-to-day changes weaken the prescription. Separate a training reference from a tested 1RM. [S03–S04] |
| RPE / RIR | Adjust effort based on performance on the day | Subjective and less precise far from failure; never use it as a pain score. Best first feedback method to implement. [S02–S04] |
| Top set + back-off work | A practical way to individualize subsequent loads | Optional after re-entry; a top set need not be heavy or maximal. Evidence does not make this a mandatory format. [S03, S06] |
| Linear progression | A simple coaching option when recent exposure is low | No obligatory increase every visit. Track symptoms and actual performance first. This is a design choice, not a proven individual recovery curve. |
| Undulating / periodized loading | Vary demands while practicing the lifts | Strength evidence in trained people favors some periodization; it does not require a competition peak or validate one branded program. [S07] |
| Minimum effective dose | Keep a workable floor during a busy week | The powerlifting study used experienced lifters and often high effort. Do not copy that intensity into re-entry. [S06] |
| Greater hypertrophy volume | Build muscle when the current dose is tolerated | Healthy-adult volume findings are an eventual reference, not a first-week quota. Count overlapping accessory work. [S01, S18] |
| APRE / rep tests | Adjust loads from achieved repetitions | Performance-test fatigue is a poor fit for early conservative re-entry. Network rankings are not proof of a best method. [S05] |
| Velocity-based training | Objective feedback with suitable measurement | Requires credible measurement and load/velocity interpretation. No need to buy equipment or treat ordinary video impressions as validated velocity data. [S04–S05] |
| Deload / easier week | A coaching tool when accumulated workload is not tolerated | A fixed calendar, a readiness score and a percentage reduction are not individually validated medical thresholds. |
| Session RPE × minutes | Describe whole-session internal load over time | Cannot identify spinal tissue stress, predict injury or certify recovery. [S19] |
| Aerobic activity | Support the owner's health goal alongside lifting | Increase from current activity and tolerance; five gym visits do not automatically satisfy aerobic recommendations. [S15] |

## Source cards

### S01 — ACSM resistance training position stand, 2026

[Publication](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/) ·
[official explanation](https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/)

Overview of 137 systematic reviews in healthy adults. Regular resistance
training improves several muscle and physical-function outcomes. Heavier
loading benefits maximal strength; more weekly volume can benefit hypertrophy.
Failure and elaborate methods are not prerequisites for useful progress.
Applicability: strong general foundation, indirect for an advanced lifter's
post-injury return. Do not turn population summaries into a starting-volume
requirement or assume all findings apply equally to competitive strength.

### S02 — Zourdos et al., RIR-specific RPE scale, 2016

[Primary validation study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26049792/)

Twenty-nine experienced and novice squatters were studied. RPE related to bar
velocity and proximity to failure. Approximate scale: RPE 10 = no additional
repetitions, 9 = one, 8 = two, 7 = three, 6 = four or more. These are estimates
under comparable technique and range of motion, not a guarantee. Useful for
feedback; not a diagnosis, measurement of pain or universal 1RM conversion.

### S03 — Helms et al., RPE versus percentage loading, 2018

[Primary training trial](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/full)

Compared RPE-guided and percentage-guided periodized training with sets and
repetitions matched in trained men. Supports studying RPE as a practical loading
method rather than treating fixed percentages as the only valid prescription.
Short duration and a small sample limit generalization. This study does not
establish rehabilitation loads or guarantee superiority for a particular owner.

### S04 — Hickmott et al., load and volume autoregulation, 2022

[Systematic review / meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35038063/)

Fifteen studies separated load prescription from velocity-loss-based volume
control. Autoregulated and standardized loads produced similar average strength
improvements; lower velocity-loss cutoffs favored strength in the comparisons
studied, while accumulating more work could favor hypertrophy. Useful rationale
for managing effort and volume separately. Not evidence for an exact fatigue
coefficient, a universal velocity threshold or an injury-prevention algorithm.

### S05 — Autoregulation network meta-analysis, 2025

[Publication](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40791980/)

Compared APRE, RPE, velocity-based and percentage-based approaches. Rankings
favored some autoregulated approaches, but squat comparisons did not show
moderate/large differences and some intervals were broad. A ranking probability
is not the probability that the owner will benefit and does not prove a single
best protocol. Use as an update alongside direct trials and S04, not as a reason
to prescribe strenuous rep tests during re-entry.

### S06 — Minimum effective dose in powerlifters, 2021

[Mixed-methods research and training interventions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34527944/)

Combined surveys/interviews with small training interventions. Low weekly doses
can improve 1RM in experienced lifters over short periods. This supports having
a manageable minimum when work becomes busy. Minimum is not optimal, and the
high-effort singles / loads used in parts of this research should not be copied
into a return after an unresolved back episode. Competition performance and
long-term health are different targets.

### S07 — Moesgaard et al., periodization, 2022

[Systematic review / meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/)

Thirty-five studies with volume equated favored periodized over non-periodized
training for 1RM, with a possible advantage of undulating over linear training
in trained participants. Hypertrophy differences were not clear. The trained
subgroup estimate was uncertain. Useful for a later strength block; not proof
that complex periodization is necessary for general fitness or that a
competition taper is needed now.

### S08 — Halonen et al., retraining after a break, 2024

[Primary trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39364857/)

Compared continuous training with a training–detraining–retraining sequence,
including a ten-week break. Some strength and muscle-size outcomes recovered
rapidly during retraining. This supports realistic optimism about regaining
adaptations. Healthy trial participants, exercises and controlled detraining
differ from an experienced powerlifter returning after back symptoms. Do not
predict the owner's recovery speed or assume tissue tolerance matches his
perceived strength.

### S09 — NICE NG59, low back pain and sciatica

[Official recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59/chapter/Recommendations)

Supports assessment for alternative causes, tailored activity/exercise and
selective imaging. MRI is not routinely required for every episode; specialist
imaging is considered when it could change management. This does not mean the
owner has been examined, has a disc herniation or is cleared for a heavy barbell
program. A clinician can assess persistent, recurring or limiting symptoms.

### S10 — JOSPT low back pain clinical guideline, 2021

[Guideline / recommendation summary](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2021.0304)

Supports active care, including appropriate trunk exercise, general exercise
and other exercise modalities according to presentation. Education and
psychosocial context matter alongside activity. There is no basis here to label
hyperextensions, rows or any single exercise a cure for an undiagnosed lesion.
Gradual, tolerable exposure and assessment of a person's presentation are more
defensible than declaring the back fragile or prescribing a universal fix.

### S11 — NHS back pain urgent-symptom guidance

[Official patient guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/)

New saddle/genital sensory loss, bladder/bowel changes and serious neurological
symptoms, including symptoms in both legs, need urgent medical assessment.
This is triage guidance, not evidence that these symptoms are present. Ordinary
muscle fatigue and focal neurological weakness are different reports; clarify
the distinction rather than inferring it from the word “weakness.”

### S12 — Garthe et al., weight-loss rate in athletes, 2011

[Randomized trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/)

Twenty-four athletes were assigned to slower or faster intended weight loss.
Slower loss better supported lean-mass and some strength outcomes during
strength training. Small, mixed-sport sample and different intervention lengths
limit precision. Supports avoiding aggressive weight loss while restoring
training, but does not establish an exact weekly target for this owner or his
calorie maintenance level.

### S13 — Morton et al., protein and resistance training, 2018

[Systematic review / meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)

Found benefit from protein supplementation when total intake was insufficient;
the modeled total-intake breakpoint was about 1.6 g/kg/day with substantial
uncertainty (upper confidence bound about 2.2). This is not a hard physiological
ceiling or direct evidence for a specific dieting prescription. Use total daily
protein and an explicit body-mass basis; do not require supplements if food
already supplies the intended intake.

### S14 — Longland et al., protein during energy restriction, 2016

[Randomized trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/)

During a short, substantial energy deficit and intensive exercise, 2.4 g/kg/day
produced better body-composition outcomes than 1.2 g/kg/day. Supports considering
protein needs during dieting separately from energy-balanced training. It does
not prove 2.4 is necessary, establish an optimum for advanced lifters, or justify
copying the trial's severe deficit and workload during injury re-entry.

### S15 — WHO physical activity guidelines, 2020

[Official guideline summary](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/9789240014886)

For adult health, the reference includes 150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic
activity (or its vigorous equivalent) per week alongside muscle-strengthening
activity. This is a population health target, not a sudden starting dose or a
requirement to add exhausting cardio to every lifting session. Record baseline
walking and aerobic activity before choosing a gradual progression.

### S16 — IOC respiratory illness consensus, 2022

[Consensus statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35863871/)

Return to training depends on illness severity, symptoms and response to
exercise; returning to participation and returning to full performance are
different stages. Useful for an illness flag and reassessment workflow. Fever,
systemic symptoms or concerning cardiopulmonary symptoms should not be handled
by mechanically decreasing a barbell weight. No single rest-day count applies
to every illness; follow treating-clinician advice when relevant.

### S17 — Wiewelhove et al., foam rolling, 2019

[Meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31024339/)

Examined acute performance and recovery outcomes. Foam rolling can offer
modest mobility / soreness benefits in some contexts. It may remain a brief
preference if comfortable, but is not established protection against disc
injury and cannot replace progressively loaded practice of the movement.
Do not infer that more rolling or stretching makes a high workload safe.

### S18 — Robinson et al., proximity to failure, 2024

[Meta-regressions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/)

Strength improved over a broad range of estimated RIR; hypertrophy appeared
more related to closer proximity to failure. RIR was estimated from study
descriptions and analyses were exploratory. Supports distinguishing strength
practice from high-effort hypertrophy work. Does not make failure mandatory or
establish an exact optimal RIR for every exercise, and does not supply a
post-injury re-entry prescription.

### S19 — Foster et al., session RPE, 2001

[Primary monitoring study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11708692/)

Validated a whole-session perceived-exertion approach against heart-rate-based
monitoring in cycling and basketball. Session RPE × duration can describe
internal load in arbitrary units. It is separate from set RIR-based RPE and is
not a measure of spinal loading, a recovery percentage or an injury prediction.
Keep the raw score and time rather than collapsing symptoms into the product.

## Provisional synthesis for this owner

These are coaching/design judgments, not outcomes established by a trial of
this individual and not an active prescription:

- Separate re-entry from later strength development. Use recent performance,
  tolerability and next-day feedback before increasing demands.
- Keep an adaptable weekly minimum. Three main sessions with up to two short
  optional sessions is a candidate matching his stated preferences; five visits
  must not become five heavy exposures or mandatory make-up work.
- Begin below previous competition workload. Retain the competition template
  as reference and count front squats, good mornings, back extensions, rows,
  pull-ups and dips as actual exposures.
- Consider easier technique practice around RPE 5–6 initially only if the
  movement is comfortable and response remains acceptable; do not calculate
  a precise e1RM from such distant-from-failure work. The exact target and dose
  still need an individual review when any movement symptoms are reported.
- A successful session plus an unremarkable next-day response can justify
  considering one small change. It does not obligate a weight increase.
- Do not stack aggressive dieting and aggressive workload increases. Establish
  logged intake and body-mass trends; the reported 83 kg / 12% is an aspiration,
  not a measured body-composition forecast.
- Prioritize a factual journal before any automated recommendation engine.
  Explain every proposed change and preserve a path to holding or reducing work.

## What remains uncertain

For an individual, movement-specific tolerable volume, current strength and
actual maintenance calories require current observations. Medical history and
reported symptoms, when relevant, require their own assessment. None can be
recovered reliably from a historical record, one workout, a video alone, a
readiness questionnaire or a universal fatigue coefficient. The owner's actual
reports and unresolved questions are kept in the private local profile.
